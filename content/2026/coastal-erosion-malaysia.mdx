## Fishyology Insight
Property on Malaysian coasts affected by erosion loses between 1 and 52 percent of its value, according to the studies that have tried to measure it (Mohsen et al., 2024; Ehsan et al., 2022). The force behind that loss is measurable too. Along the northern stretch of Peninsular Malaysia's east coast, mean annual wave power reaches 4 kW per metre of shoreline, and the northeast monsoon drives the most severe erosion between November and March (Mirzaei, Tangang & Juneng, 2014; Dong et al., 2023). Anyone who casts from the beaches and river mouths of Terengganu and Kelantan is fishing the coast the literature ranks as most exposed.

Nemo assembled this piece from a body of literature compiled and summarized via Scopus AI, then matched each claim to the study the report cites for it, by title, journal and study site. The physical science holds up well: much of it rests on field work along the Terengganu coast. Some of the report's claims about sand mining, displacement and mangrove restoration do not, because the studies attached to them come from an inland site in Selangor, from Bangladesh and from Indonesia. Where that happens, the text says so.

#### Scope and Method of the Synthesis

The report frames its scope as 2000 to 2023, with historical context, across physical geography, coastal engineering, environmental science, socio-economics and policy. Its methods include systematic reviews, meta-analysis, satellite and remote sensing data, field studies and stakeholder surveys. The citations reach further than the stated window, from a 1985 entry to a cluster of papers published in 2026.

The report names four limitations, and they govern how the rest should be read. Most findings rest on published studies, so site-specific variability may not be fully captured. Socio-economic impacts are often inferred from case studies and may not represent every coastal community. The long-term effectiveness of coastal defences under climate change remains uncertain. And indigenous knowledge and community-based adaptation are underrepresented in the research.

Nemo adds a fifth. The report lists fisheries decline among the ecological consequences of erosion, but judging by their titles, none of the studies it cites for that claim measures fish catches or fish populations. They concern the protective value of mangroves, sea level rise in a wetland park and how communities adapt. For an angling readership, that is the gap that matters most.

#### Two Coasts, Two Problems

On Peninsular Malaysia, erosion is two problems, one for each coast. The east coast has sandy beaches with steep profiles that face the northeast monsoon directly, and it erodes faster as a result. A multi-hazard coastal vulnerability index for the east coast singled out its northern districts as the highest-risk zones (Ariffin et al., 2023).

The west coast is more stable. Mangroves and estuarine sedimentation dominate, and accretion, the coast building outward, is common. Erosion there tends to be local, where development or a change in sediment supply interrupts the system. A satellite comparison of shoreline change in Terengganu and Johor, using the Digital Shoreline Analysis System, draws that contrast between the two coasts (Khurram et al., 2026).

| | East coast | West coast |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical shore | Sandy beaches, steep profiles | Mangrove and estuarine shores |
| Dominant trend | Erosion, highest in northern districts | Accretion common; localized erosion |
| Main driver | Northeast monsoon wave energy | Development and changes in sediment supply |

*Table 1. Regional contrast in coastal erosion, as summarized in the source report (Ariffin et al., 2023; Khurram et al., 2026).*

Sea level rise sits on top of both. It worsens erosion in low-lying coastal zones and raises their exposure to storm surge and flooding (Dong et al., 2023; Ariffin et al., 2023).

#### The Moon Moves Sand Too

One of the report's findings will make immediate sense to anyone who plans a trip around the tide table. On the Kuala Terengganu coast, researchers tracked seasonal changes in the beach's cross-shore profile and found that monsoon-driven water movement interacts with the lunar tidal cycle. Erosion peaked around the new moon and accretion around the full moon (Harris et al., 2024).

The study covers a single stretch of coast with a moderate tidal range, so it should not be stretched to every Malaysian beach. What it shows is that the shape of the beach is not fixed even within a single month. The slope, the gutters and the bars a shore angler reads at the water's edge are partly set by the moon phase.

#### What People Add to the Problem

The report's human drivers are coastal development, sand mining and unregulated land use. The first is the best supported. Urban growth, infrastructure and reclamation alter natural sediment flows, often without adequate environmental mitigation, and a national review of coastal reclamation in Malaysia traces its trends, drivers and impacts (Chee et al., 2023).

The sand mining claim needs narrowing. The report says sand extraction and river damming reduce the sediment reaching the coast and intensify shoreline retreat. Its Malaysian citation is a case study of sand mining at Bestari Jaya, Selangor, that addresses the mining's effects, causes and concerns (Ashraf et al., 2011), not the coastline downstream. Its other two sources are a study of sandy shores in Shandong, China, and a global review of river sand mining. The mechanism is plausible: a river that delivers less sand leaves the coast less to rebuild with. The Malaysian shoreline evidence is not in this body of work.

The report also files hillside landslides and peat soil studies under unregulated land use. They describe real hazards, but a different one, and Nemo sets them aside here.

#### Who Carries the Cost

The width of the range is the finding. Erosion's cost depends heavily on where a property sits, and the real estate survey behind it describes itself as preliminary.
</KeyFinding>

Erosion damages farmland, homes and infrastructure, and the report links it to economic loss and forced relocation. A survey of households on the west coast of Peninsular Malaysia found low perception of the risk but a high willingness to pay for adaptation measures (Ehsan et al., 2022). That combination suggests communities would back protection if someone organized it. Who is vulnerable also varies with gender composition, agriculture, education and income, and the report notes that women play a dominant role in coastal trade in Kelantan and Terengganu (Ariffin et al., 2023). Those are the same states where the wave energy is highest.

The costs are not only financial. Erosion changes a community's lifestyle and sense of identity, with education and awareness improving how people cope (Jaharudin et al., 2019). The report's sharpest social claim, that displacement breaks down community networks and reduces coping capacity, rests on a study of river erosion in Bangladesh (Islam et al., 2020). It may well hold in Malaysia; this literature has not shown it.

Local knowledge already carries some of the load. Communities in Tun Mustapha Park, Sabah, respond to beach erosion with their own measures, including mangrove planting and relocation (Saleh et al., 2022). The report's citations also include a 2026 study of reclamation's socio-economic impact on Penang's fisheries community (Jasni et al., 2026), though the report draws on it only in passing.

#### Mangroves Are Coastal Defence

Where mangroves are lost, the coast loses its natural protection along with the ecosystem services the forest provides (Saleh et al., 2022; Othman, 1994). The report states that this habitat loss affects fisheries and biodiversity, with consequences for food security and livelihoods. That chain is real, but the fisheries evidence lies outside this body of work. Fishyology's [digest on mangrove nurseries](/blog/mangroves-fish-nurseries-malaysia) covers it.

#### What the Defences Actually Do

Malaysia's coasts are lined with hard structures: seawalls, breakwaters, groynes, revetments, geotextile tubes and earth bunds (Rashidi et al., 2021). They work in the short term. They also alter how sediment moves, push erosion further along the coast, need constant maintenance and may lose effectiveness as sea levels rise (Rashidi et al., 2021). A study of shoreline change at Kuala Nerus, Terengganu, examined exactly this effect of coastal protection structures on how the shoreline evolves (Zulfakar et al., 2020).

Mangrove replanting is the nature-based alternative. Mangroves weaken incoming waves and reduce erosion, and success depends on species choice, planting density and local water movement. Survival is the weak point.

<KeyFinding stat="~30%" label="survival rate reported for replanted mangroves, which the report calls moderate." source="Hashim et al., 2010; Wiharyanto et al., 2025" image="https://res.cloudinary.com/dub3h3elq/image/upload/v1794401899/replanted_mangroves_sv00bo.jpg" imageAlt="Mangrove forest floor of dark mud covered in upright breathing roots, with young seedlings sprouting between the trunks">
The report attaches this figure to two studies, one Malaysian and one from Tarakan in North Kalimantan, Indonesia, without saying which supplies it. Treat it as indicative.
</KeyFinding>

The more promising evidence combines the two. The report describes hybrids that pair mangrove planting with sand fences or breakwaters, and says they improve sediment retention and site elevation while offering a cost-effective option for monsoon-affected coasts. Its Malaysian example is an integrated approach to mangrove rehabilitation at Sungai Haji Dorani (Hashim et al., 2010).

| Approach | What it does | What the literature flags |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hard structures | Holds the line in the short term | Down-drift erosion, upkeep, weaker under sea level rise |
| Mangrove replanting | Attenuates waves, restores habitat | Survival around 30%, needs long-term monitoring |
| Hybrid | Shelters planting while sediment builds | Long-term effectiveness unproven |

*Table 2. Coastal defence approaches compared, as summarized in the source report.*

Monitoring has improved faster than defence. Satellite methods such as the Digital Shoreline Analysis System now quantify shoreline change (Khurram et al., 2026), and the report lists drones and GIS alongside them. Governance lags behind. Environmental impact assessment is mandatory for coastal development but needs better ecological input, site-specific data and cost-benefit analysis. The report calls for strengthening Integrated Coastal Zone Management, drawing partly on an evaluation of those efforts in Penang (Albotoush & Tan, 2019).

#### What the Research Still Lacks

The report is candid about its gaps, and they cluster around time and people. Few studies follow a defence for long enough to know whether it survives rising seas and stronger monsoons (Dong et al., 2023; Ariffin et al., 2023). Vulnerability assessments rarely combine physical, socio-economic and climate data in one frame, though composite indices that map both are now emerging, including one built for the east coast (Ariffin et al., 2023). The report also flags floating breakwaters as a promising but experimentally unvalidated idea.

Socio-economic research is the thinnest strand. The effect on property values rests on a preliminary survey, and the psychological toll of losing a shoreline has barely been studied (Mohsen et al., 2024; Jaharudin et al., 2019). Fisheries do not appear in the report's list of gaps at all, which is itself telling.

#### Applied Significance for Malaysian Anglers

The research changes how to read a familiar beach. On the east coast, November to March is the erosion season, so the beach a shore angler returns to after the monsoon is not the beach they left. Within any month, the Kuala Terengganu work suggests the profile shifts with the moon. Neither finding says where the fish go, but both say the ground under them moves on a schedule.

Hard structures deserve a second look too. A new wall or groyne protects the stretch behind it and, as the literature warns, can push erosion down the coast. The beach beyond the newest rock is the one to watch.

The largest gap is the one this site cares about most. The report asserts that erosion damages fisheries, but none of its cited studies measures that. The research also lacks long-term monitoring, and it notes that incorporating local knowledge improves planning for coastal communities (Saleh et al., 2022; Said et al., 2024). Anglers return to the same beaches and river mouths year after year. Dated photographs taken from the same spot, before and after each monsoon, would be a record this literature does not yet have.

A beach can look like the most permanent part of a fishing spot. The research says it is the part most likely to have moved by next season.
